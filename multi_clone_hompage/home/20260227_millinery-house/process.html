Four stages of transformation, carried out by hands trained over years to know the precise moment when felt becomes couture.

The Métier

A Living Tradition

The word milliner — derived from Milan, the city that first exported fashionable hats to England in the sixteenth century — has always carried with it a sense of cosmopolitan refinement. At Chapeau Noir, we take that lineage seriously.

Our craft is taught mouth-to-ear and hand-to-hand. There is no manual that captures the right pressure of a thumb against steaming felt, or the exact angle at which a feather must be anchored so it moves in sympathy with its wearer's stride. These things are learned only through time and repetition, which is why every Chapeau Noir milliner undergoes a minimum of five years of apprenticeship before working on a client commission independently.

The four stages described here represent the essential arc of every hat we create — but within each stage lies a world of nuance that keeps our craft alive and evolving.

The difference between a hat and a couture hat is not the material. It is the conversation between the maker's hands and the form they are coaxing into being.
I
Stage One

The Consultation

Every commission begins not with material, but with observation. When a client enters our atelier, the milliner's eye is immediately at work — reading the proportions of the face, the set of the shoulders, the quality of light in the room. The consultation is a structured conversation, but also a quiet study.

We ask about occasion, about colour, about the client's relationship with hats past. We ask about comfort and confidence. We take precise measurements — the circumference of the head, the depth from brow to crown, the width from ear to ear across the skull — and we record them in our bespoke commission ledger, a handwritten record that accompanies every Chapeau Noir hat from first sketch to final fitting.

Full crown measurement taken with a soft tape measure and confirmed with our brass calliper gauge.

Occasion, venue, dress colour, and wearer's personal style documented in the commission ledger.

Fabric and silhouette options presented as swatches and archive photographs, not digital renders.

II
Stage Two

The Blocking

Blocking is the oldest millinery technique and still the most essential. The chosen material — fur felt, wool felt, sinamay, straw, or silk — is dampened with steam until it becomes pliable and responsive. It is then stretched over a wooden block cut to the client's exact crown measurements and pinned into position to dry.

Our workshop holds over three hundred blocks, many of them hand-carved originals dating to the nineteenth century. The choice of block shapes the personality of the hat. A shallow crown with a long front brim produces one kind of authority; a high crown with a short, sharp brim produces another. The milliner's understanding of this vocabulary is what distinguishes a hat-maker from a couturier.

Steam temperature controlled at 95–98°C for fur felt; 80–85°C for sinamay to prevent fibre damage.

Material is blocked wet and allowed to dry naturally for 24–48 hours before removal from the block.

Over 300 hand-carved blocks in our Paris workshop, supplemented by custom-turned blocks for unusual crown dimensions.

III
Stage Three

The Shaping

Once the blocked crown has dried and been removed from the block, the work of shaping begins. The brim is cut to its final width, its edge determined either freehand or by template, depending on whether the hat is catalogue or fully bespoke. Wire is then hand-stitched to the brim edge using a blanket stitch of extraordinary tightness and precision — this is the architecture that gives the brim its lift, its drape, or its dramatic upturn.

Crown and brim are then joined by hand, with a seam that is concealed by the hatband. The interior is lined in our house silk — an ivory duchess satin with the Chapeau Noir name printed in a restrained serif — and a padded petersham sweatband is hand-stitched at the crown base, fitted to the exact measurement taken at consultation.

Italian-milled millinery wire used for all brim edges — spring steel for structure, copper for soft drape styles.

Ivory duchess satin lining printed with house name in 8pt Didot. Sewn with a French fell seam for longevity.

Crown circumference verified against the commission ledger measurement at this stage before final fitting.

IV
Stage Four

The Trimming

Trimming is where the hat acquires its character — and where the milliner's artistic sensibility is most fully expressed. The hat arrives at this stage as a beautiful but quiet thing. Trimming is the act of giving it a voice.

At Chapeau Noir, all trimmings are applied by hand, using a curved millinery needle and silk thread. Feathers are hand-curled using the back of a butter knife — a technique unchanged since the nineteenth century — and anchored with invisible stitches at the quill. Ribbons are steamed and shaped before application. Silk flowers are formed petal by petal. Veiling is gathered, draped, and stitched to produce exactly the right proportion of mystery.

Feathers sourced from CITES-compliant suppliers only. All exotic feathers must carry documentation before use.

House ribbons woven to specification by Lyon's last surviving silk ribbon loom, in twelve standard widths.

Each finished hat is inspected by Madeleine Noir before leaving the workshop. Wedding hats are always inspected personally.

Materials

The Finest Sourcing

We source exclusively from heritage mills and traditional craftspeople who share our commitment to quality and ethical practice.

□

Fur Felt

Biella, Italy — Since 1887

Rabbit and hare fur felt from Lanificio di Biella, the oldest continuously operating felt mill in Europe. Our standard weight is 110g; heavy winter hats use 140g.

○

Sinamay

Manila, Philippines — Heritage Weave

Hand-woven sinamay from abacá fibre, sourced from a family cooperative in Cebu who have supplied Parisian millinery houses for four generations.

△

Straw & Sisal

Tuscany, Italy — Artisan Mills

Florentine straw braid and sisal from Signa, the historic straw-hat district of Tuscany. Available in natural, bleached, and our house-dyed colourways.

★

Silk & Organza

Como, Italy — Maison Romagnoli

Duchess satin, silk organza, and silk habotai from the last silk weaving house in Como. Used for linings, draping, and couture trimming applications.

◆

Feathers

Ethical Supply — CITES Compliant

Coque, ostrich, and pheasant feathers sourced from CITES-certified suppliers in France and South Africa. All exotic feathers carry full provenance documentation.

◯

Ribbons & Trims

Lyon, France — Last Loom Standing

Grosgrain, petersham, and velvet ribbon woven to our specifications by the last traditional ribbon loom in Lyon. Exclusive house colourways are dyed in Grenoble.
