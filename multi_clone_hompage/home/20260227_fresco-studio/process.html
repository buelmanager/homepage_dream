Five Movements,
One Masterwork

How a fresco is born — from first conversation to final carbonization, and the conservation that follows forever.

The INTONACO Method

Ancient Discipline,
Living Practice

Buon fresco — true fresco — is not a decorative technique. It is a chemical event. Mineral pigments suspended in water are applied to freshly laid lime plaster. As the plaster dries and absorbs carbon dioxide from the air, it carbonizes — and in that crystallization, the colour becomes permanently part of the wall itself.

This is why a Roman fresco, properly maintained, can survive two thousand years. The pigment is not painted onto the wall. It is alchemized into it.

"We are not decorators. We are not muralists in the commercial sense. We are chemists of colour and light, working with materials that will outlast every living thing that now stands in the rooms we paint."

— Maestro Aldo Ferrante, INTONACO Studio

The Five Movements

Each Step Is as
Irreversible as the Last

I
Movement I

Consultation
& Vision

Every INTONACO commission begins with a period of intensive study that may last from six weeks to six months. We do not accept a commission until we understand the building completely — its history, its light at every hour of every season, its proportions, its purpose, and the ambitions of the person who will live within it.

We compile an extensive brief that includes architectural drawings, historical research, iconographic studies, and pigment references. The vision that emerges from this consultation is not ours alone — it belongs to the architecture, the client, and the centuries.

  • Site visits at dawn, noon, dusk, and night
  • Archival and historical research into the building
  • Iconographic programme developed in dialogue with client
  • Full-colour presentation of proposed composition
  • Pigment palette approved before any commitment
II
Movement II

Sinopia
& Cartoon

Once the composition is approved, our painters execute the sinopia — the full-scale preparatory drawing in red ochre that is applied to the arriccio layer of plaster. In the Renaissance, the sinopia was the master's own hand; at INTONACO, the named Maestro always draws the sinopia personally.

The full-scale cartoon — a paper drawing of the entire composition — is then pricked along every contour line and pounced with charcoal dust each morning before fresh plaster is laid, transferring the design onto each day's surface with mathematical precision.

  • Full-scale sinopia drawn in situ by the named Maestro
  • Cartoon produced at 1:1 scale on specialist cartridge paper
  • Pouncing with willow charcoal dust — no digital tools
  • Each day's transfer checked against the sinopia by the Maestro
III
Movement III

The Intonaco
Laying

The intonaco — the final plaster layer — is our studio's name and our governing discipline. Each morning, plasterers lay only as much fresh intonaco as the painters can complete that day before the surface begins to set. This unit is called the giornata: the day's work.

Our plaster formula follows the traditional lime-and-marble-dust preparation. We source lime from kilns that supply the Opificio delle Pietre Dure and marble dust from the same quarries used by Florentine studios in the 15th century. No synthetic additives are ever used.

  • Traditional aged lime slaked for minimum 12 months
  • Carrara marble dust: 200-mesh and 400-mesh
  • Applied in two layers: arriccio and intonaco
  • Each giornata planned the evening before for the following dawn
  • Surface worked with wooden floats — no machine application
IV
Movement IV

Pigment
Application

Our painters work into the damp intonaco with natural mineral pigments suspended in pure water. No binding agent is required: the carbonization of the plaster creates the permanent bond. Every colour decision is final. Every brushstroke is committed to for centuries.

We use exclusively mineral and earth pigments — no synthetic dyes, no modern acrylics, no compromises. Our pigment laboratory, directed by Maestro Elena Vasquez, sources and prepares every hue. Many of our pigments come from the same mines that supplied Raphael, Michelangelo, and Piero della Francesca.

  • Lapis lazuli from Afghanistan — ultramarine of antiquity
  • Azurite from central European deposits
  • Malachite green, hand-ground in the laboratory
  • Yellow, red, and brown ochres — pure iron oxides
  • Burnt sienna and raw umber from Umbria and Cyprus
  • Vine black and bone black for shadow and depth
V
Movement V

Documentation
& Conservation

When the final giornata is complete and the plaster has fully carbonized, our work is technically finished. But our responsibility to the commission has only reached its first milestone.

Every INTONACO commission is subject to a comprehensive documentation process: photographic record, spectroscopic pigment analysis, written layer-by-layer description, and a formal condition report. All documentation enters our conservation archive and a copy is provided to the custodian. We carry a three-generation conservation guarantee on all commissions.

  • Ultra-high-resolution photographic documentation
  • X-ray fluorescence pigment analysis of all major colours
  • Written sinopia and plaster layer documentation
  • Conservation care protocol for the custodian
  • Triennial inspection offered for all commissions
  • Three-generation conservation guarantee, notarized
The Pigment Palette

Colours That Cannot Fade

Mineral pigments fixed in lime plaster are among the most stable substances known to conservation science. The following pigments form the core INTONACO palette.

Lapis Lazuli
Badakhshan, Afghanistan

The most precious pigment in the Renaissance palette. Ground from the semi-precious stone, used for skies, robes of the Virgin, and celestial passages. Unchanged in colour for six centuries.

Malachite
Democratic Republic of Congo

Copper carbonate hydroxide, hand-ground to varying particle sizes. Coarser grinds produce deeper greens; finer grinds produce paler, more luminous tones.

Red Ochre
Umbria, Italy

Natural haematite from Umbrian deposits — the same iron oxide used in cave paintings 40,000 years ago. The most lightfast pigment available to any painter.

Yellow Ochre
Roussillon, France

Natural iron oxyhydroxide from the famous Roussillon deposits. Warm and luminous, it forms the foundation of skin tones, stone architecture, and warm light passages.

Burnt Sienna
Siena, Italy

Raw sienna earth calcined at 800°C produces this warm, transparent reddish-brown. Essential for shadow passages and the glazing techniques of the later fresco tradition.

Vine Black
Tuscany, Italy

Carbonized grapevine charcoal, producing a cool blue-black of extraordinary richness. Used for the deepest shadow passages and the underpaint sinopia transfer.

Bianco di San Giovanni
Florence, Italy

Traditional Florentine lime white — slaked lime dried and reground — used as the principal white pigment. Its lime base bonds naturally with the intonaco plaster.

Green Earth (Terra Verde)
Verona, Italy

Celadonite from Veronese deposits — the classic Trecento underpaint for flesh tones. In pure fresco, it reads as a cool neutral green used extensively in architectural passages.

The Daily Discipline

The Giornata:
Dawn to Dusk

The giornata — literally, "the day" — is the fundamental unit of fresco painting. Each morning, the Maestro plans exactly how much can be painted before the plaster sets. Too little ambition and the work proceeds too slowly; too much and the plaster dries before the pigment can be fixed.

This daily reckoning is both a practical discipline and a philosophical one. It makes the painter honest. Every morning begins with the knowledge that what is not finished today cannot be recovered.

5:30 AM
Morning PlanningMaestro reviews the giornata area with the head plasterer. Light quality assessed. Area marked.
6:30 AM
Intonaco LaidPlasterers apply fresh intonaco to the giornata area. Carbon dioxide monitoring begins.
7:30 AM
Cartoon TransferThe Maestro pounces the cartoon onto the fresh plaster surface with willow charcoal.
8:00 AM
Painting BeginsPigment application from the lightest values to darkest. The Maestro never stops to deliberate — only to decide.
1:00 PM
Midday CheckSurface carbonization assessed. Pace adjusted if the plaster is setting earlier than anticipated.
5:00 PM
Work CeasesThe plaster has set. The day's work is permanent. Tomorrow's giornata is planned before leaving the scaffold.
