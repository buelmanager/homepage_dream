How We Work

The Art of Sacred Process

Four stages, hundreds of hours, one enduring piece

The Four Stages

From Conversation to Creation

Every commission is a shared undertaking. We move at the pace of meaning, not the pace of production. Here is how a piece comes into being.

I
Stage One

The Consultation

Every piece begins with listening. A commission at BRODERIE opens not with a design brief but with an unhurried conversation — conducted in person at the atelier, or via a private video consultation. We ask about the story behind the commission, its intended recipient, the emotions it should carry, and the space it will inhabit.

From this conversation, our lead artisan drafts an initial concept document: a narrative description of the proposed work, a colour palette drawn from our silk thread library, and an estimated timeline. This document is the contract of understanding between studio and client.

"The best commissions begin before the needle ever touches cloth. They begin in the telling."
II
Stage Two

Design & Transfer

The approved design is drawn by hand at full scale on tracing paper. Our artisans work from botanical illustrations, archival reference materials, and the client's own photographs or heirlooms. No designs are digitally generated; every line is drawn with pencil by the hand that will later stitch it.

Transfer to the ground fabric is accomplished using the prick-and-pounce method — the oldest and most accurate technique, unchanged since the Renaissance workshops of Florence. The design is perforated with a fine needle, then pounced with charcoal or chalk dust through the perforations onto the stretched linen. The resulting dots are connected with a watercolour pencil that will eventually be concealed by thread.

No digital printers, light boxes, or iron-on transfers ever enter this studio. The design lives in the hand before it lives in cloth.
III
Stage Three

The Stitching

With the design on the frame, the stitching begins. Depending on the commission, one or more of our master artisans will work the piece, always under the direction of the lead artisan. We stitch in natural daylight wherever possible; artificial light, however precise, distorts the perception of colour and texture in thread.

A small wall piece might require two hundred hours of stitching over six weeks. A large botanical study can take eight hundred hours across twelve months. The client receives progress photographs at each significant stage — never out of obligation, but because we believe the making is itself a gift worth witnessing.

We work at most four to five hours of stitching per day per artisan. Concentration and the quality of attention degrade after this. We do not push through fatigue; every stitch reflects the state in which it was made.
IV
Stage Four

Framing & Delivery

The completed embroidery is laced onto a conservation-grade stretcher bar, a technique that allows the textile to breathe and expand without damage over centuries. It is then framed under museum-grade UV-filtering glass in a frame selected in consultation with the client — from our archive of hand-gilded and ebonised options.

Every completed commission is accompanied by a signed Certificate of Provenance, a detailed materials record listing every thread colour, source, and dye lot, and a care guide for the next hundred years. The piece is packed in archival tissue and a custom crate for delivery by specialist art courier, insured to the replacement value of the commission.

The certificate travels with the piece. Should it ever be sold, gifted, or bequeathed, the next owner will know exactly who made it, when, and with what intent.
Stitch Vocabulary

Techniques We Master

Silk Shading
French tradition

The most painterly of all embroidery techniques. Long-and-short stitches layered across dozens of colour values create photorealistic gradation. Used for botanical studies, portraits, and landscape commissions.

Master level
Goldwork
English & Japanese tradition

Metal threads — purl, passing thread, check thread, and plate — are couched or manipulated onto the ground fabric. The resulting surface catches light with a three-dimensional intensity no other technique can approach.

Master level
Sashiko
Japanese tradition

A running stitch technique from the Edo period, originally used to reinforce textiles and create warmth. The geometric patterns — tortoiseshell, hemp leaf, wave — carry centuries of symbolic meaning.

Advanced
Broderie Anglaise
French-English tradition

Delicate pierced and embroidered whitework, characterised by eyelet holes and satin stitch. The defining technique of heirloom bridal linen and the technique from which our studio takes its name.

Master level
Hardanger
Norwegian tradition

Cutwork and drawn-thread whitework on an even-weave Hardanger ground. Kloster blocks, dove's eyes, and bars create an architectural lattice of extraordinary precision. The technique of Sigrid Bakke's speciality.

Advanced
Kalocsa Folk
Hungarian tradition

Vivid polychrome embroidery from the Kalocsa region of Hungary. Traditional rose, tulip, and carnation motifs executed in hundreds of interlocking satin stitches, creating a dense, jewel-like surface.

Advanced

Conservation & Framing

Built for Three Centuries

The framing of a completed embroidery is as considered as its making. We work only with conservation-grade materials — no acids, no adhesives, no contact with anything that could compromise the textile over time.

1
Lacing onto Stretcher Bars

The finished textile is laced with strong linen thread onto a kiln-dried stretcher bar, allowing the fabric to breathe and adjust to seasonal humidity changes without distortion.

2
Acid-Free Mounting

A conservation-grade mounting board, pH-neutral and buffered, is placed behind the stretched work. No adhesives contact the textile at any point.

3
Museum Glass

99% UV-filtering Tru Vue Museum Glass is fitted, blocking the wavelengths most damaging to dyes and fibres. A spacer bar keeps the glass from touching the stitching surface.

4
Frame Selection & Backing

A frame is chosen in consultation — from hand-gilded gesso to ebonised oak. The back is sealed with archival backing board, signed, dated, and accompanied by the provenance label.
