The Philosophy

No Machine
Can Replicate This

Every piece made at Terra Forma passes through the same sequence of hands, decisions, and elemental forces that has defined our practice since 1985. The process is slow by design. We consider urgency to be incompatible with quality at the level we pursue.

"We do not make pots. We release objects that were always waiting inside the clay."

From the quarry to the gallery, a single vessel may take six months to reach its final form. Most of that time is spent waiting — drying, curing, deciding, and refusing to hurry. We have rejected thousands of pieces at every stage of the process. What remains on our shelves has earned its place there.

What follows is the complete account of how we work — not as a marketing exercise, but as a statement of accountability to the people who collect our work.

Stage by Stage

The Making of
a Terra Forma Piece

01
Clay Sourcing

The Earth

We source our clay from three distinct quarries in the British Isles, each chosen for its specific mineral character and the qualities it lends to the finished work. No blended commercial body. No shortcuts. The clay we use today is the clay we have always used.

Each batch is tested through at least two full firing cycles before it is approved for production. We maintain meticulous records of every batch — its quarry, its water content, its shrinkage rate, and its behaviour under different firing conditions. Clay is not a commodity to us. It is an ingredient with a biography.

  • Devon red earthenware — warm iron-rich body, ideal for terracotta and slip-trail work
  • Cornish kaolin porcelain blend — translucent when thin, exceptionally pure white firing
  • Scottish Highlands stoneware — dense, frost-resistant, heavy mineral signature
  • Each batch hand-wedged to remove air pockets before any wheel work begins
  • Minimum 7 days conditioning before first throw
02
Wheel-Throwing

The Wheel

All forms are thrown by hand on traditional kick-wheels — not electric motors. The kick-wheel operates at a speed determined entirely by the maker's foot, creating a rhythm of work that is responsive, intimate, and impossible to replicate with a machine.

A piece may be thrown, collapsed, and re-centred dozens of times before the maker is satisfied that the form is correct. We do not use measuring tools on the wheel. The form must feel right before it is allowed to dry. Walls are pulled to their minimum possible thickness consistent with the function and scale of the work.

  • Traditional kick-wheel — speed controlled by foot, not motor
  • No templates or guides — form determined by maker's hand and eye
  • Each piece thrown fresh — not press-moulded or slip-cast
  • Trimming and foot-ring cutting performed when leather-hard (24–48 hours after throwing)
  • Pieces rejected at this stage: approximately 30% of all thrown forms
03
Drying & Bisque Firing

The Wait

Once thrown and trimmed, each piece dries slowly on open wooden boards in the studio — never force-dried. The drying process can take two to four weeks depending on the thickness of the work and the season. We have no dehumidifiers. The studio's natural humidity is part of the process.

A bisque firing to 950°C is then performed in an electric kiln to consolidate the clay and prepare it for glazing. This is the only stage in our process that uses an electric kiln. Everything before and after depends on hand, patience, and flame.

  • Natural drying — 14–28 days depending on thickness and season
  • Bisque kiln: 950°C, approximately 8 hours
  • Bisqued pieces inspected individually — cracks, warping, or structural weakness rejected here
  • Rejection rate at bisque stage: approximately 15% of surviving thrown pieces
04
Glazing

The Surface

Our glaze library contains forty-seven individual glaze recipes, each developed and refined in the studio over the forty years of our practice. We do not purchase commercial glazes. Every glaze is mixed by hand from raw materials — feldspar, silica, whiting, iron oxide, cobalt, ash — weighed to the gram and recorded in our recipe archive.

Application is by dipping, pouring, and brushing — never spraying. The thickness of the applied glaze is critical: too thin and the surface will be dry; too thick and it will run and obscure the form. This judgement is made by eye and touch, developed over years of practice. It cannot be taught quickly.

  • 47 in-house glaze recipes — none purchased commercially
  • All glazes mixed from raw mineral materials by studio staff
  • Application: dipping, pouring, brushing — no spray guns
  • Glaze thickness measured by touch and visual inspection — no instruments
  • Selected pieces intentionally left unglazed — natural ash deposit in kiln provides surface finish
  • Wax resist and latex resist used for selective bare clay areas
05
Anagama Kiln Firing

The Fire

The final and defining stage of every Terra Forma piece: 72 hours of continuous wood-firing in our anagama kiln at temperatures reaching 1,300°C. The kiln is loaded by hand in a placement that takes one full day, with each piece positioned according to where the flame will pass it and what effects the position will produce.

During the firing, the kiln is tended continuously — stoking the firebox, monitoring the temperature at six points within the chamber, managing the draft and the ash clouds that drift through and deposit on the surfaces of the work. The flame is the final artist. We arrange the conditions and feed the fire. The rest is collaboration.

  • 72-hour continuous wood-firing — oak and ash logs sourced from managed woodlands
  • Peak temperature: 1,280°C–1,320°C depending on placement position
  • Kiln team of 3 potters working in 8-hour shifts throughout the firing
  • Natural ash deposits from combustion create unique glaze surfaces on unglazed work
  • Reduction atmosphere created by managing air inlet — produces iron blush, celadon tones
  • Cooling over 3 full days before kiln is opened
  • Post-firing rejection rate: approximately 20% — kiln mark pieces accepted as highest value works
06
Inspection & Finishing

The Edit

Opening the kiln three days after the firing is complete is a ritual that never loses its power. Each piece is lifted from the kiln and inspected — first quickly, for structural integrity, then slowly, for the quality of its surface and the character of its marking. Only then is a decision made: gallery, rejection, or occasionally the kiln's highest honour — designation as a Kiln Mark piece.

Pieces approved for the gallery receive a final light grinding of the foot-ring to ensure they sit flat and steady. No other intervention is made. We do not refire, reglaze, or alter pieces after their emergence from the anagama. What the kiln returns to us is what we offer to the world.

  • 72-hour cooling before kiln opening
  • Individual inspection by Eleanor Whitmore or senior studio potter
  • Three classifications: Gallery / Rejected / Kiln Mark (rarest designation)
  • Foot-ring grinding — the only post-firing intervention permitted
  • All gallery pieces signed, dated, and documented with clay body and firing records
  • Photography and archival documentation before any piece leaves the studio
The Anagama

The Heart of
Everything

Our anagama kiln was built in 1991 by Eleanor and James Whitmore with the help of the master kiln builder Tomoo Hamada — son of the legendary Shoji Hamada. It took eleven weeks to construct, using over four thousand hand-cut firebricks and a clay-and-sand mortar mixed on site. It has fired continuously ever since.

The anagama (Japanese: "hole kiln") is the most demanding and unpredictable kiln form in ceramic practice. Unlike a controlled electric kiln, the anagama creates conditions that cannot be precisely replicated — variations in temperature within the chamber, ash cloud patterns, and reduction effects that are influenced by wind direction, wood moisture content, and the accumulated history of previous firings embedded in its walls.

This unpredictability is not a limitation. It is the source of the work's character. Every piece that emerges from an anagama bears evidence of something that happened to it — the record of a specific fire on a specific day, never to be repeated.

Raw Materials

Where the Clay Comes From

Three distinct clay bodies from three distinct corners of the British Isles. Each with its own geological history, mineral fingerprint, and character in the kiln.

Devon Red
Devon, South West England

A warm, iron-rich earthenware quarried from the red sandstone beds of Devon. High iron content produces warm terracotta tones in firing and allows exceptional slip-trail decoration. The body that defines our earthenware collection — intimate, warm, and suited to domestic use.


Firing range: 1,050°C – 1,150°C · Colour: Warm red-orange · Best for: Terracotta, slip-trail, platters, bowls

Cornish Kaolin
Cornwall, South West England

A fine-particled porcelain blend based on Cornish kaolin — one of the purest sources of kaolinite in Europe. When thrown thin, light passes through the walls. The white fire and translucency make this the body of choice for our most refined vessel work and the Hands Series.


Firing range: 1,240°C – 1,320°C · Colour: Pure white-grey · Best for: Translucent vessels, fine bowls, sculptural work

Highlands Stoneware
Scottish Highlands, Scotland

Dense, frost-resistant stoneware from the igneous-influenced soils of the Scottish Highlands. Heavy in mineral content — traces of mica, feldspar, and occasional quartz inclusions that create a characteristic texture under a matte glaze. The body most at home in the anagama kiln, responding dramatically to ash deposits and flame marking.


Firing range: 1,250°C – 1,320°C · Colour: Warm grey-brown · Best for: Anagama, sculptural, large vessels

Our Standard

Why It Takes
This Long

The question we are most often asked is why our waiting lists are so long. The honest answer is that we cannot work any faster without compromising what makes the work worth waiting for.

A Terra Forma commission takes a minimum of six months from conversation to delivery — and frequently twelve to eighteen. A single firing cycle — preparation, loading, 72-hour fire, three-day cool, unloading — takes nearly two weeks. And before the firing, the piece may have spent months on the drying shelves, waiting.

We believe that the time invested in a piece is inseparable from its quality. Objects made slowly, with patience and refusal to compromise, carry something in their surfaces that rushed work cannot acquire. Our collectors understand this. They are not buying a product. They are commissioning time.

No Production Runs

We do not produce editions of more than eight of any piece. Most works are unique. This is not a marketing strategy — it is a consequence of how the kiln works. No two firings produce identical results.

Right of Refusal

Eleanor Whitmore reserves the right to reject any piece at any stage — including after firing. Pieces that do not meet our standard are destroyed, not discounted. This has cost us significant income. It has preserved our reputation.

Full Provenance

Every piece leaves the studio with a written record of its clay body, glaze recipe, firing date, position in the kiln, and maker. This is not a certificate — it is a biography. We believe collectors deserve to know what they are holding.
