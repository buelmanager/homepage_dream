Our Heritage

Nine Centuries
of an
Unbroken Line

"The oldest bond
between man
and raptor."

TALON was not founded — it was inherited. The estate and the mews at Aldric were already ancient when the first written record of training was made in 1147. Since then, through war and plague, reformation and revolution, one thing did not change: the birds flew every morning from these stones.

Today we carry that lineage with rigorous purpose. Our twelve master falconers each trained under the previous generation, in an unbroken chain of transmission. We do not teach from books — we teach from memory and experience, exactly as our predecessors did.

UNESCO designated falconry as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010, recognising it among the world's most significant living traditions. We have been its custodians long before that recognition, and we will continue long after.

Chronicle

A History in Dates

1147
Foundation of the Mews

Lord Aldric de Falco receives a gyrfalcon from King Inge I of Norway. The stone mews at Aldric Estate are constructed to house the first birds. The training lineage begins.

1219
The Falconers' Codex

Third Master Falconer William de Aldric compiles the first written manual of the school's methods — forty-seven pages on manning, feeding weights, and field technique. Three copies survive in museum collections.

1398
Royal Commission

By appointment of Henry IV, the school supplies and trains birds for the royal mews at Windsor. This relationship continues — with interruptions — for three centuries.

1651
The Dark Years

Following the execution of Charles I, estate lands are sequestered. Master Falconer John Crane moves six birds across the border to Scotland, preserving the bloodline. The estate is restored in 1660.

1892
First Public Training

For the first time in its history, the school admits students from outside the estate. Eight gentlemen of means complete the inaugural Introductory Course. Demand exceeds capacity within three seasons.

2010
UNESCO Recognition

Falconry is inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. TALON is cited as one of eleven schools whose practice informed the designation documentation.

Today
The Living Tradition

34 birds. 12 master falconers. Four programs accepting students year-round. The tradition not as museum piece but as living, evolving mastery — precisely as Aldric de Falco intended.

Chief Master

Edmund Crane
XIV

Edmund Crane is the fourteenth in the unbroken succession of Master Falconers at Aldric. He received his first bird at the age of nine — a male kestrel named Corvus — from his grandfather, the thirteenth master. He has not been without a bird since.

He holds the International Falconers' Association Master Certificate, the UNESCO Practitioner designation for falconry heritage transmission, and has advised the British Museum on its medieval hawking artefact collection. He lectures twice yearly at Oxford.

A Living
Humanity

Falconry was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List in 2010, then expanded to 24 nations in 2016. The designation recognises it not as a sport or entertainment but as a living cultural practice — a form of knowledge and relationship transmitted between generations. TALON is one of the world's oldest continuous practitioners of this heritage.
