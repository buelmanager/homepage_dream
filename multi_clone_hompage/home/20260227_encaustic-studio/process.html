The Method

Fire,
Wax, Light

The encaustic process has not changed in two thousand years. Beeswax. Damar resin. Mineral pigment. Heat. Each layer fused into the one below, building a surface that glows from within.

01
Foundation

Beeswax Preparation

Raw beeswax arrives in large blocks from organic apiaries. It is first filtered through muslin cloth to remove impurities, then melted slowly in a double-boiler at no more than 80°C. Overheating destroys the wax's natural properties.

Once melted, damar resin crystals are added — typically at a ratio of one part damar to eight parts wax by weight. The resin raises the melting point and improves adhesion and hardness. The blend is stirred until fully incorporated, then poured into working pots.

Wax Type Cold-pressed organic beeswax, unbleached
Temperature Melt at 63–65°C. Never exceed 80°C
Resin Ratio 1 part damar : 8 parts wax by weight
Panel Preparation Baltic birch ply, 18mm, sealed with rabbit-skin glue size
02
Colour

Pigment Suspension

Dry mineral and earth pigments are weighed and stirred into the molten wax medium. Each pigment behaves differently — some disperse immediately, others require extended mixing to fully saturate the wax. Heavy pigments (such as lead white or vermilion) must be stirred continuously to prevent settling.

The studio maintains forty individual pigment pots, each labelled with its mineral source, particle size, and approximate working properties. The palette has not changed in years.

Earth Pigments Yellow ochre, red ochre, raw umber, burnt sienna, caput mortuum
Mineral Pigments Cobalt blue, lapis lazuli, malachite, verdigris, ultramarine
Special Pigments Egyptian blue, carbon black, lead white (safety-handled)
03
Application

Layering and Fusing

Each layer of pigmented wax is applied to the panel with a natural-bristle brush. The wax must be applied hot — if it cools before contact with the panel, it will not bond. After each layer is applied, it is fused using a heat gun or torch, held in motion to avoid burning.

Fusing is not just adhesion — it is transformation. The heat merges each new layer into the one below, creating translucent depth. Multiple thin layers produce far greater luminosity than a single thick application.

Application Temp Wax applied at 70–75°C. Panel pre-warmed.
Fusing Tool Heat gun at 80–90°C or propane torch in constant motion
Layer Count 12–40 layers per finished work. Each fused before the next.
Timing Each layer must fully cool and harden before fusing the next
04
Completion

Burnishing and Finishing

The final surface is burnished by hand using a soft cotton cloth. This friction generates a controlled warmth that brings out the characteristic encaustic sheen — not a high gloss, but a deep, living luminosity that shifts as the viewer moves.

Some works receive additional surface work: incising (drawing through wax layers to reveal colour below), collage (embedding gold leaf, linen thread, or pigment dust), or selective re-fusing to alter texture in specific areas. The work is complete when it no longer asks for anything more.

Burnishing Tool Soft cotton muslin — no pressure, only gentle heat
Surface Options Matte, satin, or burnished high-polish finish
Varnish None required or applied. Wax is self-protecting.
The Toolkit

Instruments of Heat

🔥

Propane Torch

For fusing and dramatic surface movement. Held in constant motion — never stationary — to fuse without scorching.

🌬️

Heat Gun

For gentle, controlled fusing of large areas. Allows more precise temperature management than open flame.

🪛

Heated Stylus

A thermostatically controlled metal tool for fine detail work, line drawing, and incising through wax layers.

🖌️

Natural Bristle Brushes

Hog hair and badger bristle brushes. Synthetic bristles melt at wax temperatures. Replaced frequently.

Technical Reference

Temperature Guide

Encaustic painting is a temperature-sensitive process. Too cool and the wax does not bond; too hot and it loses structure. The following guide applies to standard beeswax-damar blend at an 8:1 ratio.

Temperature State Application
55–60°C Softening point Wax becomes pliable. Not yet workable as a liquid medium.
63–67°C Melting point Ideal working temperature. Wax flows smoothly from brush to panel.
70–80°C Fusing range Heat gun or torch temperature for layer-to-layer fusion without burning.
80–90°C Maximum safe Limit for damar-blended wax. Exceeding this may cause resin to separate.
Above 200°C Flash point Wax ignites. Studio must be ventilated. Fire suppressor always present.
Permanence

The Most Archival
Medium on Earth

The Fayum mummy portraits, painted in Roman-era Egypt over two thousand years ago, remain vivid today. No other medium — not oil, not tempera, not acrylic — can make the same claim. Encaustic wax does not yellow, crack, or fade. It does not require varnishing or treatment.

A CEREUS panel is built to last not decades but centuries. The materials used are identical to those used in antiquity. The archival quality is not a marketing claim — it is a material fact.

No YellowingBeeswax does not oxidise or yellow over time, unlike linseed oil in oil paint.
No CrackingThe wax surface remains flexible and does not crack from thermal expansion the way oil on canvas does.
No Varnish NeededThe wax surface is self-protecting. No varnish, sealant, or UV-blocking coating is required or recommended.
No Glass RequiredEncaustic panels do not need to be framed behind glass. The surface breathes and should remain open to the air.
