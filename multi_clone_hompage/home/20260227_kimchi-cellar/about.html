Four Generations
of Living Tradition

The Lee family has tended onggi pots in Jeonju's clay-rich earth since 1947. What began as a family practice of survival has evolved into a devotion to fermentation as art form, science, and cultural legacy.

Lee Byung-jun
4th Generation
The Master

Son-mat — The Taste
of Living Hands

"My grandmother taught my father. My father taught me. Not from a book — from watching, from smelling, from tasting. The onggi remembers everything. We only have to listen."

Master Lee Byung-jun inherited the cellar in 1996 at age 28, after 12 years of apprenticeship under his father. He has since expanded the cellar from 80 to 300 onggi pots, and introduced a systematic approach to fermentation science while preserving every ancestral technique.

He holds the rank of Certified Traditional Food Artisan (전통식품명인) from the Korean Ministry of Agriculture, one of fewer than 80 individuals nationwide to hold this distinction in fermented vegetables.

Our History

A Living Archive
of Korean Fermentation

1947
Lee Sang-chul builds the first underground cellar

Post-liberation Jeonju. Lee Sang-chul, a master onggi potter, excavates a 2-metre chamber beneath his family home and places the first 12 pots. He makes kimchi for his village through the winter famine years, establishing the foundational baechu kimchi recipe still used today.

1962
Second generation — cellar doubles in size

Lee Jae-won deepens and extends the cellar to 3.5 metres and 40 pots. He begins systematic labelling of each onggi by batch date, seasonal ingredients, and ambient temperature — a practice that creates ONGGI's living archive of 60+ years of fermentation data.

1979
Partnership with onggi potter Choi Sung-won

A decade-long collaboration begins with Gyeonggi-do master potter Choi Sung-won, who creates a proprietary onggi clay body with higher porosity coefficient than standard stoneware — perfect for the slow, cold fermentation of ONGGI's cellars. The relationship continues with Choi's son today.

1994
Inaugural Jeonju Kimchi Festival — ONGGI as anchor

The first Jeonju Kimchi Cultural Festival selects ONGGI as its flagship exhibitor. Master Lee Jae-won demonstrates onggi fermentation techniques to over 4,000 visitors. The cellar gains national recognition and begins supplying select Seoul restaurants.

1996
Fourth generation takes the cellar

Lee Byung-jun returns from Seoul, where he had studied food science at Yonsei University, and takes formal charge of the cellar at age 28. He bridges traditional son-mat with modern microbiology, tracking lactic acid bacteria populations through seasonal cycles.

2013
UNESCO recognition — kimjang inscribed

The United Nations recognises kimchi-making (kimjang) as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. ONGGI is cited in the Korean government's nomination documentation as a preservation model for traditional fermentation techniques and onggi use.

2019
Expansion to 300 onggi pots, cellar deepened to 4.2m

A major expansion deepens the cellar and creates three temperature zones. The 300-pot capacity allows simultaneous aging at 4°C, 6°C, and 8°C — enabling new kimchi varieties with precisely calibrated fermentation profiles. The cellar experience tour programme launches.

2024
Korea Traditional Food Artisan designation

Master Lee receives the government's highest recognition for traditional food craft, joining an elite group of 80 artisans nationwide. The designation includes a duty to document and transmit traditional techniques — ONGGI begins formal apprenticeship programmes.

What We Stand For

Our Commitments
to the Craft

01

No Shortcuts, Ever

Every batch of kimchi at ONGGI is made by hand, salted with West Sea tidal salt, mixed with gochugaru ground on-site, and aged in earthen cellars for the minimum time required by the recipe — never less, regardless of demand.

02

Heritage Vegetables

We grow or source only Joseon-era heritage varieties of napa cabbage, radish, cucumber, and chili from certified farms in North Jeolla Province. No hybrid cultivars selected for yield over flavour are accepted into the cellar.

03

Living Culture Archive

We maintain 12 distinct kimchi cultures — some propagated continuously for over 80 years — as a living archive. Samples from each onggi generation are documented, preserved, and made available to food researchers and cultural institutions.

04

Education and Transmission

ONGGI accepts one formal apprentice every three years. Our cellar workshop programme has introduced kimchi fermentation to over 3,000 visitors since 2019. We believe that a tradition not transmitted is a tradition already lost.
