ANFT-Based Protocol

How the
Walk Unfolds

No destination. No fitness goal. A structured sequence of invitations designed to return you to yourself.

"In Shinrin-yoku, the guide does not lead you somewhere. The guide simply creates the conditions for the forest to do what it has always done — and gives you permission to receive it."

— Kenji Nakamura, Founder & Lead Guide

The Protocol

Six Phases of
Forest Immersion

Our walks follow the ANFT (Association of Nature and Forest Therapy) guide protocol, adapted over ten years of practice to the specific rhythms of our old-growth forest. No two walks are exactly alike — the forest is always different — but this arc remains constant.

01

Arrival

The Threshold Crossing

We begin before we enter the forest. At the forest edge — the literal threshold between the world of obligation and the world of living things — your guide leads a brief grounding practice. Devices are silenced. Schedules are released. We breathe together. The nervous system begins its shift before the first step under the canopy.

15 minutes
02

Opening

The Mapping of Senses

The first invitations are simple: What do you hear? What moves in your peripheral vision? What does the air taste like here, in this place, at this hour? Your guide does not tell you what to notice. The invitations open a door; you choose what to walk through. Many guests describe this phase as the moment their internal monologue first quiets.

20–30 minutes
03

Deepening

The Wandering

With senses now more open, we enter a period of unstructured wandering within a defined area of forest. There is nowhere to be, nothing to find. The guide is present but not directing. Guests often stop at unexpected things — a pattern in bark, the weight of a particular silence, the way a patch of light moves through the canopy. The forest begins to meet you where you are.

30–45 minutes
04

Stillness

The Sit Spot

Each guest finds their own sit spot — a place in the forest that calls to them — and settles there alone for twenty minutes of unstructured silence. No instructions. No goal. Just the forest and you. This is the phase where guests most often report what they describe as a profound sense of belonging, of being received by something larger than their individual experience.

20 minutes
05

Integration

The Council Circle

Guests return from their sit spots and gather in a circle. The guide offers an open invitation: to share what arose, or to sit in companionable silence. There is no pressure to speak. What is shared is received without commentary or response. Often, what is expressed is not what guests expected — the forest has a way of surfacing what has been waiting.

20 minutes
06

Closing

The Forest Tea Ceremony

Every walk closes with a hand-gathered forest tea — pine needle in winter, cedar tip in spring, wild mint in summer, rosehip in autumn. The tea is prepared slowly, deliberately, by the guide, while guests sit in the closing quiet of the forest. The tea ceremony is not an activity. It is a sacrament. A way of saying: we were here. The forest held us. We are grateful.

20–30 minutes

Sample Invitations

The Language
of Forest Therapy

Invitations are the core tool of a forest therapy guide. Unlike instructions, they are open-ended — they suggest a direction without requiring a destination.

"Find something in this forest that feels like it has been waiting for someone to notice it. Spend time with it. Listen to what it has to say."

— Sensory deepening invitation · Used in Phase 3

"Let your feet find a place to settle. Not the right place — simply a place. Notice what the ground receives from you. Notice what you receive from the ground."

— Sit spot opening invitation · Used in Phase 4

"What is moving in this forest right now? Not what you think should be moving — what you actually perceive moving, at the edge of your attention."

— Peripheral awareness invitation · Used in Phase 2

"Before we leave, let yourself say goodbye to one thing in this forest — silently, or aloud if it comes. It has held you today. Let it know you noticed."

— Threshold departure invitation · Used in Phase 6

✦ ANFT Certified Practice

The Standard

Why ANFT
Certification Matters

The Association of Nature and Forest Therapy established the first standardized evidence-based training for forest therapy guides in North America. ANFT certification requires 200+ hours of supervised practice, deep knowledge of forest ecology, and ongoing continuing education.

SHINRIN holds ANFT certification for all guides, plus Wilderness First Responder (WFR) credentials and trauma-informed practice training from the Hakomi Institute. When you walk with us, you are in professionally trained hands — and the hands of the forest itself.

Not all forest therapy guides are equal. Many use the term without training. We encourage guests to always ask about credentials, supervision, and protocol basis before walking with any provider.

Common Questions

Before You
Walk With Us

Do I need to be physically fit to participate?

+

Our walks cover very little ground — often under one mile in three hours. We move at a fraction of hiking pace. Most experiences are accessible to guests with limited mobility, though we ask you to share any needs in advance so we can design the experience accordingly.

What if I find silence uncomfortable?

+

This is more common than you might expect, and our guides are trained to work with it. The discomfort of silence is itself useful information — and the forest is extremely good at filling silence with enough sound that it never feels empty. Many guests who arrive most resistant to silence leave most transformed by it.

Can I bring my children?

+

Children ten and older are welcome in our Half-Day Forest Bath. For younger children, we offer private family walks on request — these are designed differently and move at the pace of the youngest child present. Contact us to discuss what would work best for your family.

What happens if it rains?

+

We walk in rain. Our forest is a temperate rainforest and is, in many ways, most alive in wet weather. The phytoncide release from wet bark and leaf litter is significantly higher than in dry conditions. We provide details on appropriate rain gear in your booking confirmation. Cancellations due to weather are rare and only occur in conditions of lightning or ice.

Is this spiritual or religious in nature?

+

Shinrin-yoku originated in Japan where Shinto reverence for nature is deeply embedded in culture, but our practice is secular and evidence-based. We do not impose any spiritual framework. What you encounter in the forest is entirely your own. Many guests report experiences that feel spiritual to them — and many do not. Both are equally welcome.

How do I get to the forest?

+

Our forest sanctuary is located approximately 45 minutes southwest of Portland, Oregon. Detailed directions, parking instructions, and meeting point coordinates are provided upon booking confirmation. We also offer a limited shuttle service from downtown Portland for groups of four or more.
