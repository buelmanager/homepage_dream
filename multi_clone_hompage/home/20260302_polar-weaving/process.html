The Process

From Tundra to Textile

How a SVALBORD Piece is Born

01

Fibre Selection in the Field

Each spring, our fibre specialist walks with the Svalbard reindeer herds across the tundra as they migrate between winter and summer grazing grounds. At peak growth — usually late May — the animals begin naturally shedding their winter undercoat.

Hand-combing rather than shearing preserves the structure of the underhair. Only the finest fibres — those with the lowest micron count and highest lustre — are selected for SVALBORD use.

6 kgRaw Fibre per Grand Throw
02

Hand Spinning

Cleaned and carded fibre is spun by hand on traditional drop spindles — the same tools documented in Viking-era archaeological records from Norway. The process is slow by design: hand spinning produces a yarn with variable twist that catches light differently along its length, giving SVALBORD textiles their distinctive luminous quality.

Machine-spun yarn, no matter how fine, cannot replicate this. The irregularity is the beauty.

3 WeeksSpinning per Throw
03

Natural Dyeing

SVALBORD uses only natural dyes derived from arctic and subarctic botanical sources — lichen for grey and rust tones, crowberry for deep blue-black, cloudberry for warm amber, and arctic willow bark for tawny gold. No synthetic dyes are used at any stage.

Seasonal variation in the plant harvest means no two batches are exactly identical. A throw commissioned in July will have slightly different tonal qualities than one commissioned in October — a variation we embrace rather than suppress.

04

Weaving

The weave itself — from first warp thread to final selvedge — is the work of a single master weaver who signs the finished piece. We do not rotate weavers mid-piece. The structural vocabulary of each individual weaver — their tension, their rhythm, their pattern memory — is the authorship of the work.

A Grand Throw requires 280 hours of loom time. During this period, the weaver works in the studio without music. The pattern is oral — recited, not read from a chart.
