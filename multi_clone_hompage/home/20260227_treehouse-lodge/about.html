How It Began

A Forest That
Asked to Be Kept

In the winter of 2017, Clara and Edmund Harwick walked into a valley of ancient oak and found a forest that no one had touched in decades. The trees were enormous. The ground was a mosaic of moss, fern, and fallen limbs thick with lichen. Owls had reclaimed the dark. Deer moved through like shadows at dusk.

Clara, a conservation arborist, and Edmund, a structural engineer who had spent a decade restoring medieval timber frames, looked at each other. They had been searching for a place to build — not the kind of building that replaces what is there, but the kind that reveals it.

They purchased the estate in March 2018. The first treehouse — a modest structure in a bifurcating oak — was completed by autumn of that year. It was never meant to be a business. It was a proof of concept: that you could sleep inside a living system and leave it more whole than you found it.

"We did not design around the trees. The trees designed around us. We simply listened long enough to hear the difference."

— Clara Harwick, Co-Founder

Word spread through the conservation community. Then through architecture circles. Then through a single photograph published in a magazine, taken from the deck of the Canopy Suite at sunrise, which caught the forest in a way that made people feel something they couldn't quite name.

By 2022, twelve treehouses had been completed. Not a single tree had been felled.

The Founders

Shaped by Wood & Wind

Clara Harwick

Co-Founder · Conservation Arborist

Clara holds a doctorate in forest ecology from the University of Edinburgh and has spent fifteen years working with the Woodland Trust to protect ancient tree populations. At CANOPY, she leads all ecological decisions — from which fallen timber can be used to how the walking paths are maintained so as not to compact root systems. She lives on the estate with her two daughters and a collection of identification guides she can no longer fit on any shelf.

Edmund Harwick

Co-Founder · Structural Engineer

Edmund trained as a structural engineer before spending a decade restoring historic timber-frame buildings across northern England. His obsession with joinery — the way wood wants to meet wood — led him to design each treehouse as a bespoke structural conversation with its host tree. No two structures are alike. Each reflects the personality of the tree it lives within. Edmund is rarely found indoors before noon and refuses to carry a phone.

The Canopy Chronicle

How the Forest Grew

Winter 2017
The First Walk

Clara and Edmund discover the estate — 800 acres of untouched ancient woodland in Herefordshire. They walk it every day for three weeks before making an offer.

Spring 2018
Estate Acquired

Purchase completed. Ecological survey commissioned. The decision is made: no felling, no ground break, no machinery larger than a wheelbarrow will enter the woodland.

Autumn 2018
First Treehouse Complete

The Canopy Suite — then known simply as "the oak room" — is completed entirely by hand. Edmund sleeps in it alone for a week to understand what it requires.

2019
Soft Opening

Three treehouses open to guests — by private invitation only. The response is immediate and unanimous. The forest changes people.

2020
Conservation Partnership

Formal partnership established with the Ancient Tree Archive. Seed banking programme begins. CANOPY becomes carbon-negative by year-end.

2022
Twelve Treehouses

The full collection of twelve singular treehouses is complete. Each one named by the tree it inhabits, not the other way around.

2026
A Thousand Trees

The reforestation programme surpasses 6,000 native trees planted since founding. The ancient woodland now breathes with new growth at its edges.

What We Believe

The Canopy Principles

I.

Take Only What Falls

Every timber, every stone, every material used in our structures was already offered by the forest. We do not ask. We wait, and the forest provides.

II.

Leave No Trace Upward

Traditional no-trace ethics apply to the ground. We extend them skyward: no cables through living wood, no brackets in cambium, no light that disturbs nocturnal ecology.

III.

Silence as Amenity

We do not offer television, WiFi, or alarm clocks. We offer the sound of the forest at 3am. This is a technology no engineer has ever improved upon.

IV.

Food as Forest Story

Every meal served at CANOPY tells the forest's story: foraged mushrooms, estate honey, herbs from the clearing, fruit from the orchard we are gradually rewilding.

V.

Guests Who Come Ready

We select guests who understand that the forest asks something of them in return. Presence. Patience. A willingness to be changed by what is around them.

VI.

Permanence Over Trend

We build for centuries. The treehouses will outlast us. They are already part of the forest's memory. We are merely passing through.
