The Method

From Skin
to Scripture

Every Scriptorium manuscript passes through six distinct stages over months or years. Each stage demands specific materials, specific tools, and specific knowledge accumulated only through long practice.

The Way of the Scriptorium

Slow Work, Permanent Result

The medieval illuminator did not hurry. The monastery scriptorium operated under the understanding that a manuscript was a collaboration across time — that those who would read it centuries hence deserved the same reverence as the patron who commissioned it. We hold this understanding as fundamental. Our process takes as long as the work demands. We will not rush a commission. The finest manuscript you will ever own should not be made quickly.

Below we describe each stage in the detail we believe every patron deserves to understand. Your manuscript is not a product — it is a made thing, and the making of it is part of its meaning.

Raw Materials

The Substance of Permanence

◈
Vellum

Calf or kidskin prepared by specialist English parchment-makers using the traditional limebath and lunellum methods.

◈
Quill & Reed

Primary flight feathers from turkey, goose, or swan, cured in sand and cut to profile with a quill knife before each session.

◈
Iron-Gall Ink

Ground from Aleppo oak galls, iron vitriol, and gum arabic. Formulated to the Theophilus recipe: acidic, penetrating, permanent.

◈
Gold Leaf

23¾ carat transfer and loose gold leaf, applied over gesso or gum ammoniac ground, burnished with an agate stone.

◈
Pigments

Lapis lazuli, malachite, vermilion, lead white, and bone black. Ground on a glass slab and bound in gum arabic.

Six Stages

The Complete Method

I

Vellum Preparation

Raw skin arrives from the tannery scraped to a preliminary thickness. We continue this work ourselves: stretching the skin on a frame, scraping further with the lunellum until the surface is smooth and consistent, and pumicing with fine-grit stone to open the surface for ink absorption. The process takes two to three days per skin, and multiple skins are prepared simultaneously.

"The skin must neither drink the ink too greedily nor refuse it entirely. The pumicing creates a third surface: neither skin nor paper." — Studio notes, 2007
II

Quill Cutting & Ruling

Flight feathers — turkey for general work, swan for the finest formal hands — are first cured in hot sand to harden the barrel. A quill knife then cuts the point: an oblique nib for the Roman and italic traditions, a straight cut for Insular scripts. Nib width is calibrated precisely to the script and the body height of the text. The ruled lines — established with lead point or dry-point on a template — are transferred to each folio before writing begins.

Each quill provides approximately four hours of writing before the tip deteriorates and must be re-cut. A full Book of Hours may require sixty or more quills.
III

Calligraphic Transcription

The text is written with iron-gall ink at a pace dictated by the script: Gothic Textura demands a slow, measured stroke; Italic moves more quickly. No corrections are possible on vellum — blotted or misdirected strokes cannot be erased without damage visible under ultraviolet light. Sheets with errors are set aside for practice; the commission receives only flawless work. Spaces for illuminated initials and miniatures are left blank.

A calligrapher working on a Book of Hours may write for five to six hours per day at most. The physical concentration required makes longer sessions counterproductive.
IV

Gilding & Gold Sizing

Raised gilding begins with a gesso ground: a compound of white lead, chalk, sugar, glue, and Armenian bole, built up in five to eight coats over each area to be gilded. When dry, the surface is polished smooth with a dog's tooth burnisher. On the day of gilding, the gesso is breathed upon to activate its moisture — gold leaf, held on a gilder's tip, is laid, pressed gently through a piece of glassine, and then burnished with an agate stone until it mirrors the studio.

Flat gilding, applied with gum ammoniac, requires different timing: the adhesive must be at precisely the right degree of tackiness — a window of perhaps ninety seconds.
V

Illumination & Painting

Pigments ground on glass are bound in gum arabic at a consistency that allows thin, translucent washes. Painting begins with broad underpaint — earth tones, lead white — then builds layer by layer through weeks or months: shadows deepened with transparent glazes, highlights raised with lead white or shell gold, final details added with a brush reduced to two or three hairs. The process cannot be hurried: each layer must be dry before the next is laid.

Lapis lazuli is the last pigment applied. Its high refractive index means it reads differently under different layers and must be applied directly to its final surface.
VI

Binding & Delivery

Completed folios are collated, folded into quires, and pressed for several days to flatten them before sewing. The sewing structure — Coptic, Byzantine, or medieval chain-stitch — is chosen for its historical appropriateness to the commission. Oak boards, prepared in the studio, are covered in alum-tawed pig leather or dyed calf. Metal clasps are fitted. The finished work is wrapped in archival tissue and placed in a bespoke oak presentation casket lined with silk.

We offer a documentation pack with every commission: photographs at each stage of production, a written record of all materials and methods, and a certificate of authenticity signed by the lead illuminator.
Commission Timeline

The Arc of a Commission

Months 1–2
Consultation

Initial correspondence, meetings, iconographic programme drafting, material selection, contract and deposit.

Months 2–4
Preparation

Vellum preparation, ruling, quill-cutting, ink-making, pigment-grinding, preliminary drawing and colour tests shared with patron.

Months 4–12
Transcription

Calligraphic work. Progress photographs shared at each quire completion. Patron review at mid-point.

Months 10–20
Gilding & Illumination

Gold sizing, gilding, painting — multiple review stages. Second interim payment at start of gilding.

Final Month
Binding & Delivery

Sewing, binding, casing, final inspection, photography, documentation pack, and casket delivery. Balance payment on completion.

Frequently Asked

Questions from Patrons

Can I choose the script for my commission? ↓
Yes. We offer the full range of 23 historical scripts, from Uncial and Insular Half-Uncial through Carolingian Minuscule, Gothic Textura Quadrata, Rotunda, Humanistic Minuscule, and Italic. In consultation we will recommend the script most appropriate to your text, period preference, and aesthetic sensibility — but the final choice is yours.
Do you work in languages other than English? ↓
We have worked in Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Irish, Welsh, French, German, Italian, Spanish, Arabic, and Coptic, among others. We can transcribe any language that uses a script within our range. For non-Roman alphabets, we require the patron to supply a verified text or to engage a specialist translator of their own; we will not work from machine-translated copy.
How do payments work? ↓
Commissions are structured across three payments: a deposit of 30% on signing, a second payment of 40% at the start of the gilding stage, and the balance of 30% on completion and before delivery. All payments are in British pounds sterling by bank transfer. No work is released before the final payment is received.
Is it possible to visit the studio? ↓
Studio visits are possible by appointment for commissioned patrons at the consultation and mid-commission review stages. We do not receive walk-in visitors. The working studio is a place of concentration and we must protect that condition for the quality of all commissions in progress. We do, however, conduct thorough virtual consultations by video call, sharing materials, samples, and progress documentation in real time.
How should a finished manuscript be cared for? ↓
Vellum manuscripts should be stored at a stable relative humidity between 45% and 60% and away from direct light. The oak casket provided with each commission is designed for this purpose. When handling, clean cotton gloves should be worn. We provide a full care guide with each commission and are available by correspondence for advice throughout the life of the piece.
