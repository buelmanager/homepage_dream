About Cavalier

140 Years on
the Same Land

The Ainsworth family has trained horses and riders on this Cotswolds estate since Colonel William Ainsworth first purchased it in 1884. Four generations, one philosophy: the horse is always the teacher.

Our Story

From Cavalry
to Classical Mastery

Colonel Ainsworth established the estate as a training centre for cavalry horses in 1884. The transition to civilian riding came gradually, as the cavalry diminished and the sport of equestrian competition grew. By 1932, his daughter Dorothy had transformed Cavalier into a training academy focused entirely on classical horsemanship.

Dorothy Ainsworth was ahead of her time: she introduced dressage training to the Academy decades before it became fashionable in England, insisting that classical riding was not a continental affectation but the foundation of all good equestrian work.

The Academy today occupies the same 280 acres, maintains the same stables, and holds the same conviction: that the deepest understanding of riding comes not from technique alone but from sustained attention to what the horse is trying to say.

What We Believe

Three Convictions
of the Saddle

01

The Horse is Always Right

If the horse resists, the rider must first ask why — not correct what. Resistance is information. It is the horse's only language for saying something is wrong, unclear, or painful.

02

Progress Cannot Be Hurried

Every movement in riding must be prepared before it is asked. Asking a horse for something it is not yet physically or mentally ready to give is not training — it is a request for conflict.

03

The Land Matters

Our 280 acres are an essential part of the education. Hacking across open Cotswolds land, negotiating varied terrain and weather, builds a quality of riding that an arena alone cannot provide.
