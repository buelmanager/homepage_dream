Commission Work
How It's Made

From Grove to Gallery

Five stages of transformation — each one an act of devotion to the material. No shortcuts. No machines where hands will do. No compromise.

The Philosophy of Making

Slow Work is True Work

The entire process from grove to finished piece takes a minimum of four months. Most complex commissions require six to eight months. This is not an inefficiency — it is the essential nature of bamboo craft done properly.

Bamboo cannot be rushed. Cut before maturity, it splits unpredictably. Dried too quickly, it warps. Woven under tension, it cracks at the joints. Every stage has its own patience requirement, and TAKE has built a practice around respecting those requirements absolutely.

What you receive at the end of this process is not simply a well-made object. It is an object shaped by time — time of the bamboo's growth in the grove, time of the craftsman's attention, and time of the workshop's discipline.

01
Stage One

Harvesting

We harvest exclusively from five family-managed groves across Kyoto and Shiga prefectures. The timing is everything. Master Yamamoto follows the traditional practice of lunar harvesting — selecting stalks for cutting during the waning moon, when starch content in the bamboo is at its lowest. This significantly reduces susceptibility to insect damage after cutting.

Only stalks that have reached a minimum of three years of growth are selected, and only those scheduled for natural thinning — bamboo that would otherwise crowd out its neighbours. The harvest is conducted by hand saw, never mechanised, and each selected stalk is assessed individually before cutting.

  • Lunar-calendar timing: waning moon phase only
  • Minimum stalk age: 3 years from shoot emergence
  • Grove: Arashiyama, Higashiyama, Lake Biwa region
  • Tool: Hand saw only — no chainsaw or power tools
  • Annual harvest window: October to February
Duration: 1–3 days per harvest
02
Stage Two

Drying

Freshly cut bamboo contains up to 50% water by weight. If used immediately, the cells will continue to shrink unevenly during the making process, causing warping, cracking, and joint failure. Slow natural drying — called kanso — allows the cell structure to stabilise uniformly.

At TAKE, stalks are stored vertically in the open yard of the Higashiyama atelier, bound loosely in bundles that allow air circulation. The yard has a north-facing orientation that prevents direct summer sun, which would dry the outer surface too rapidly.

  • Duration: 60–90 days minimum (longer for thick-walled Moso)
  • Method: Vertical storage, natural air circulation, no heat
  • Orientation: North-facing yard, indirect light only
  • Test: Tap sound changes from hollow to solid-ring when ready
  • Moisture target: Below 12% before splitting
Duration: 60–90 days
03
Stage Three

Splitting

Splitting bamboo is the most technically demanding stage of preparation. A single stalk must be split into anywhere from eight to sixty-four strips of equal width, each with consistent thickness, using only a warigo (splitting knife) and a wooden mallet. No mechanical splitters are used — the human hand can feel variance in the stalk's wall that a machine cannot.

Each strip is then planed and scraped to achieve a uniform cross-section. The outer green skin (the epidermis) and inner white membrane (the endodermis) are managed carefully — the ratio of each in the finished strip determines the flexibility and strength of the woven piece.

  • Tool: Traditional warigo knife + wooden mallet
  • Split count: 8–64 per stalk depending on strip width required
  • Planing: Three-blade kanna planer for thickness uniformity
  • Width tolerance: ±0.3mm across all strips in a batch
  • Waste: All offcuts preserved for secondary works
Duration: 1–5 days per basket-equivalent
04
Stage Four

Weaving

TAKE's weaving vocabulary draws from over 200 documented Japanese patterns, some of which date to the Heian period. The fundamental patterns — yotsu-me (four-eye square), mutsu-me (six-eye hexagonal), yarai (diagonal wave), and ajiro (woven lattice) — have hundreds of variations when combined with different strip widths, tensions, and angular offsets.

Master Yamamoto considers the weaving stage a form of extended meditation. The hands must work from memory and sensation, not calculation. A basket of medium complexity requires between forty and eighty hours of weaving time. During this stage, the strips are kept lightly damp to maintain flexibility — too dry and they crack; too wet and they slip out of register.

  • Pattern vocabulary: 200+ documented Japanese weave patterns
  • Strip moisture: Maintained at 18–22% during weaving
  • Weaving time: 40–200+ hours per piece
  • No templates or jigs — all forms built by hand tension
  • Edge binding: Traditional rattan and bamboo winding
Duration: 2–12 weeks per piece
05
Stage Five

Finishing

Finishing at TAKE means preservation, not decoration. No lacquer, no paint, no synthetic coating is used. Instead, the completed piece is rubbed with tsubaki — camellia oil pressed from seeds grown in the Shizuoka region. This natural oil penetrates the bamboo fibre, imparts a subtle amber warmth, and creates a protective barrier against humidity changes.

The finished piece is then stored upright for a final resting period of at least two weeks before inspection. Final inspection involves measuring all dimensions against the commission specification, examining every joint and edge, and assessing the piece's character. Each completed work is signed by Master Yamamoto in ink on the base, and a provenance certificate — recording material origin, patterns used, and completion date — is prepared for the collector.

  • Finish: Tsubaki camellia oil, two applications
  • Rest period: Minimum 14 days post-finishing
  • Signature: Ink, hand-applied by Master Yamamoto
  • Certificate: Provenance document included with every piece
  • Packaging: Museum-grade acid-free tissue and paulownia wood box
Duration: 3–4 weeks
Material Guide

The Bamboo Species We Use

Each bamboo species has a different character. Choosing the right material for each piece is itself an act of craft.

Madake
真竹 — True Bamboo

The most widely used species in Japanese craft tradition. Long, straight internodes with thin walls make it ideal for fine splitting into delicate strips. Colour ranges from pale yellow-green to warm gold. Strong and resilient — a classic choice for baskets and vases.

Used for: Baskets, vases, tea whisks
Moso
孟宗竹 — Giant Bamboo

Japan's largest bamboo species, with culms reaching 20m in height and 14cm in diameter. Thick-walled and extraordinarily strong — the material of choice for furniture and structural applications. Its mass gives finished pieces a satisfying weight and longevity.

Used for: Furniture, heavy screens, structural elements
Kurotake
黒竹 — Black Bamboo

Naturally dark — its distinctive black-purple colouring deepens over years, not through staining. A rarer species, sourced exclusively from Kyoto's highland groves. Used for tea ceremony utensils and statement vases where visual drama is required.

Used for: Tea utensils, accent vases, collector pieces
Hachiku
淡竹 — Thin Bamboo

Finer-walled than Madake with a bluish-grey cast that ages beautifully to silver. Particularly flexible — ideal for open-weave patterns that require the strips to bend at acute angles. Its natural texture creates a more tactile finished surface.

Used for: Open-weave baskets, decorative screens
Hotei Chiku
布袋竹 — Buddha's Belly

Named for its swollen, irregular internodes that resemble the belly of the laughing Buddha. Rarely used for fine work — its value lies in its sculptural character. A single culm of Hotei Chiku, properly harvested and oiled, needs nothing added to it.

Used for: Display pieces, sculptural accent works
Mousou
毛竹 — Fine Hair Bamboo

A regional variant found in the hill groves above Higashiyama. Fine-grained, with a silky internal surface that responds exceptionally well to the planing stage. Produces strips of remarkable uniformity — prized for the most intricate weave patterns.

Used for: Fine-weave exhibition pieces, chasen tea whisks
Instruments of the Craft

Traditional Tools, Used Daily

Every tool in the TAKE workshop has been used — some for decades. None of them require a power source.

  • Warigo — Splitting Knife
    割り子

    A wedge-shaped blade of high-carbon steel, used with a wooden mallet to split bamboo stalks along their natural grain. The bevel must be maintained to within 0.5 degrees or the splits diverge unpredictably. Master Yamamoto's warigo is 38 years old.

  • Kanna — Planing Tool
    鉋

    A traditional Japanese hand plane with a single laminated blade. Used to smooth and dimension bamboo strips to consistent width and thickness. Set upside-down in a bench mount, strips are drawn across the blade rather than the blade across the strip.

  • Sakari — Awl
    錐

    A fine steel awl used to open the weave at precise points during construction, allowing new strips to be threaded under existing ones. Essential for complex pattern work. The tip geometry determines how clean the insertion point is.

  • Kikuri — Binding Needle
    菊利

    A curved, blunt-tipped needle used to thread rattan cord through the completed edge binding. Its curve allows it to navigate the tight angles of finished rim structures without damaging adjacent strips.

  • Nomi — Chisel Set
    鑿

    A set of twelve chisels in graduating widths, used for joinery work in furniture pieces. The joints in TAKE furniture use no metal fasteners — all structural connection is achieved through mortise-and-tenon joinery cut with these tools and locked with rattan binding.
