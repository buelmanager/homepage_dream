Our Story

Three Generations, One Living Tradition

The Yamamoto family has been shaping bamboo in Kyoto for over sixty years — a lineage of patience, precision, and profound respect for living material.

Master Kenji Yamamoto — Third generation bamboo craftsman

Master Craftsman

Kenji Yamamoto

Kenji Yamamoto was born in 1974 in Higashiyama, Kyoto, the third generation of a family that has worked bamboo since his grandfather first arrived from Kagoshima in 1962. By the age of twelve, he was already apprenticed under his father, learning to feel the difference between a stalk cut in the right season and one taken too early.

He studied formally under Living National Treasure bamboo artist Shokosai V in Osaka during his twenties — an experience he describes as the true beginning of his understanding. It was there he learned that bamboo was not simply a material; it was a dialogue between the craftsman's intention and the plant's inherent character.

Today, Kenji leads a team of four at the TAKE atelier, taking on no more than twelve commissions per year. He is a nominee for the Agency for Cultural Affairs' recognition of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

"Every split of bamboo is a decision. Make the wrong cut and the material resists. Make the right one, and it opens like water finding its path."

— Kenji Yamamoto

The Lineage

A Chronicle of Craft

1962

Founding — Takeshi Yamamoto

Kenji's grandfather Takeshi arrives in Kyoto from Kagoshima, establishing a small workshop in Higashiyama producing utilitarian baskets for Nishiki Market vendors. The name TAKE — bamboo in Japanese — is chosen for its simplicity and truth.

1978

Second Generation — Haruki Yamamoto

Takeshi's son Haruki takes over the workshop, shifting focus from utilitarian basketry to exhibition pieces. He wins his first award at the Kyoto Craft Biennale in 1983, establishing the family name in Japanese art circles.

1994

Kenji's Apprenticeship

Kenji, age 20, joins his father full-time and travels to Osaka to study under Shokosai V. He spends three years immersed in the formal traditions of higotake — fine split bamboo work — which will define his signature style.

2005

TAKE Atelier Reopened

Kenji restores a Meiji-era machiya townhouse in Higashiyama and reopens TAKE as a dedicated atelier. The first commission-only model is established, limiting output to ensure the highest quality of attention per piece.

2019

Kyoto Intangible Cultural Heritage

The Kyoto Prefecture recognises TAKE's weaving techniques as an example of endangered traditional craft deserving active preservation. Three apprentices join the studio under a government-supported mentorship programme.

2026

Agency for Cultural Affairs Nomination

Kenji Yamamoto is nominated as a candidate for recognition as an Intangible Cultural Heritage holder — the highest honour available to a living craftsperson in Japan. The family tradition continues.

Place & Heritage

Rooted in Kyoto's Bamboo Culture

Kyoto has been the spiritual centre of Japanese bamboo craft for over four centuries. The Arashiyama bamboo grove — visible from the atelier's hillside — remains one of the most spiritually charged landscapes in Japan, a place where bamboo reaches skyward in columns of living light.

The city's artisan districts — Higashiyama, Nishiki, Gion — have always maintained a delicate web of craft traditions: lacquerware, silk weaving, ceramics, and bamboo. Each tradition sustains the others. TAKE sources bamboo exclusively from groves managed by traditional forestry families who have grown timber bamboo for generations.

The machiya townhouse that houses the TAKE atelier was built in 1908 and retains its original engawa porch, inner garden, and high workshop ceilings. Working in such a space is not incidental — the building participates in the making of each piece.

Responsibility

The Mottainai Principle

Mottainai — the Japanese philosophy of zero waste — is not a marketing position at TAKE. It is the foundation of every decision from grove to gallery.

林

Grove Stewardship

We partner with five family-managed bamboo groves across Kyoto and Shiga prefectures, harvesting only what is scheduled for thinning. Bamboo grows back to full height within four to five years, making it one of the world's most regenerative materials.

零

Zero Waste Process

Every fragment finds purpose. Large offcuts become tea stirrers and chopstick rests. Shavings fuel the workshop kiln used for finishing pieces. Even the bamboo water pressed from freshly split stalks is collected and composted into the garden.

永

Works Built to Last

Every TAKE piece is built with a century in mind. We use no synthetic adhesives or finishes — only traditional camellia oil, rice paste, and hand-twisted rattan cord. Many pieces owned by collectors for twenty years have grown more beautiful with age.
