The Making

From Pricking to Finished Lace

Handmade bobbin lace follows an unchanged sequence of steps, each demanding complete attention and acquired skill. There are no shortcuts. The process is the work.

I

Pattern Pricking

Every piece of bobbin lace begins with a pattern — a grid of dots that maps the precise path of every thread. This pattern is transferred to stiff card by pricking each dot with an awl, creating a field of tiny holes through which pins will later be set to hold the work in position.

The spacing of the pricking determines the density and scale of the finished lace. A finer pricking — dots closer together — produces finer lace, requiring a proportionally finer thread. This relationship is absolute: thread weight, pricking density, and bobbin count must match exactly.

Historic patterns are preserved in our archive on paper, some over a century old, transferred from piece to piece as the studio passes between generations.

Materials at This Stage
  • Stiff card (120–160gsm) or pricking board
  • Steel awl, pricking wheel
  • Original pattern on tracing paper
  • Carbon or chalk transfer medium
Typical duration: 2 — 6 hours per pricking
II

Bobbin Winding

Bobbins are paired — each thread that enters the lace is held on a pair of bobbins. The thread is wound from a reel or skein onto the bobbin's shank, then the two bobbins of each pair are joined with a half-hitch at the top of the working thread. This joining knot determines the working length of the thread pair.

For a standard Bruges collar, between forty and eighty pairs may be needed — eighty to one hundred and sixty individual bobbins. The weight and feel of each bobbin matters: our studio bobbins are hand-turned from fruitwood, weighted to match the thread tension required by the pattern.

Thread Weights Used
  • Belgian linen no. 60 — standard Bruges work
  • Belgian linen no. 100 — fine Torchon
  • Japanese silk no. 80 — Chantilly & Venetian
  • Egyptian cotton no. 50 — Honiton grounds
Typical duration: 1 — 4 hours depending on bobbin count
III

Pillow Setup

The lace pillow — a firm, drum-shaped cushion traditionally filled with sawdust or sand — is the working surface of bobbin lace. The pricked pattern card is pinned to the pillow, and the wound bobbin pairs are hung from anchor pins set above the starting edge of the pattern.

The arrangement of bobbins on the pillow is not random: each pair occupies a specific position that determines which threads will cross next. The lacemaker reads the pattern not merely as a visual guide but as a sequence of movements, internalised through thousands of hours of practice until the hands work faster than conscious thought.

Pillow Types
  • Bolster pillow — Belgian and English tradition
  • Flat pillow — Torchon and geometric work
  • Mushroom pillow — circular and medallion patterns
  • Slant pillow — contemporary ergonomic use
Typical duration: 30 — 90 minutes to set up
IV

Working the Pattern

This is the lace itself: the sequence of crossings, twists, and plaits that produces the structure. There are two fundamental moves — the cross (two threads exchange positions) and the twist (a single thread loops over its neighbour) — and from these two moves, every lace ground, motif, and filling in the entire tradition is built.

In Bruges Flower lace, a continuous braid threads through the pattern, producing the flowing, serpentine character of the tradition. In Torchon, regular diagonal crossings create the geometric ground. In Honiton, individual motifs are worked separately on a small mushroom pillow and then assembled — each butterfly wing a discrete act of work.

A skilled lacemaker will complete between two and five centimetres of progress in an hour, depending on the complexity of the pattern. Working sessions of three to four hours are standard; longer sessions risk accumulated tension in the hands and inconsistencies in thread tension that become visible in the finished piece.

Key Technical Elements
  • Cross: fundamental thread exchange
  • Twist: thread tension and structure
  • Plait: braid formation for braids and grounds
  • Pin setting: holds work at each junction
  • Tallies / leaves: filled motif elements
  • Picots: decorative edge loops
Typical duration: The majority of total making time
V

Joining and Finishing

When the pattern is complete, all pins are removed in sequence — the piece must not be distorted by removing pins out of order. Threads are tied off with reef knots, trimmed close, and the knots eased into the structure where they will be invisible. Any joining threads — used when attaching Honiton motifs to grounds, or when connecting sections of long borders — are woven in by needle.

The finished lace is then washed in distilled water at room temperature, never wrung or agitated, and pinned to a blocking board to dry in its correct dimensions. Only after blocking is the piece considered finished and ready for inspection.

Quality Inspection Points
  • Thread tension consistency throughout
  • Picot regularity at edges
  • Symmetry of paired motifs
  • Absence of visible knots or joins
  • Dimensional accuracy against specification
Typical duration: 2 — 8 hours
The Tools

Instruments of a Four-Century Craft

Bobbin lace requires few tools, but each must be of precise quality. The lacemaker's relationship with her tools is intimate; many of the instruments in our studio have been in continuous use for generations.

◆
The Pillow

A firm cushion, traditionally filled with compressed sawdust or sand. The working surface of all bobbin lace. Ours are made by a single craftsman in Bruges who has supplied the studio since 1991.

◆
Bobbins

Hand-turned from fruitwood — pear, apple, or cherry. Weight and balance affect thread tension. Our bobbins are weighted with pewter spangles, the traditional decoration of English and Flemish lacemakers.

◆
Pricking Awl

A fine steel awl for transferring the pattern to the pricking card. The gauge of the awl tip must match the thread weight: a coarser awl hole would distort the thread path in fine linen work.

◆
Pins and Pins

Fine steel pins, never brass, set at precise angles to hold the work. A single row of Bruges braid may require thirty pins per five centimetres. We source exclusively from a single English pin manufacturer.

The Thread

Material is Everything

The thread used in handmade lace is not simply raw material — it is the carrier of the entire tradition. The feel, weight, twist direction, and fibre composition of a thread determines what lace can be made with it.

Belgian Linen — No. 60/100

The primary thread for all Bruges and Torchon work. Woven from flax grown in the Lys valley, it has a slight natural irregularity that gives Flemish lace its characteristic warmth.

Japanese Silk — No. 80

For Chantilly and Venetian Point. Reeled silk from Kyoto, extraordinarily fine and consistent. Its lustre is visible in the finished piece and cannot be replicated with cotton or linen.

Egyptian Cotton — No. 50

For teaching and Honiton ground work. Its tight twist and predictable tension make it ideal for learners, and its durability makes it practical for pieces intended for handling and use.

Ecru Linen — Heritage Grade

Undyed, unbleached linen for historically authentic pieces. Its natural ecru colour deepens slightly with age, as historic laces confirm. Used for museum commissions requiring period accuracy.

Commissioning a Piece

How a Commission Unfolds

From your first inquiry to delivery of the finished piece, every Dentelle commission follows a clear, transparent sequence with regular updates.

01
Inquiry

Submit your vision via the contact form or email. Include purpose, dimensions, and any references.

02
Consultation

Elena or Sophie will respond within five working days with questions, material options, and initial thoughts.

03
Proposal

We provide a written proposal: design sketch, thread specification, price, and timeline. Revisions welcome.

04
Deposit

A 40% deposit confirms the commission and schedules a lacemaker. Work begins within two weeks.

05
Progress

Monthly photographic updates as the work progresses. Opportunity to make minor adjustments at the midpoint.

06
Delivery

Finished piece, archival certificate, care guide, and making record delivered in bespoke packaging.
