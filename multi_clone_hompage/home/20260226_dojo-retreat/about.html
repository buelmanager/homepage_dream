The Temple

Three Centuries
of Unbroken Practice

What is now MUSHIN Retreat was built in 1682 as a satellite temple of the great Nanzen-ji complex in Kyoto's Sakyo Ward. Commissioned by a retainer of the Tokugawa shogunate, it served as a study center for samurai seeking to deepen their spiritual practice alongside their martial training — a fusion that was, in Edo Japan, unremarkable.

The temple passed through several stewards across the Meiji Restoration and the turbulence of the 20th century. Its remote location in the cedar hills of Ohara kept it largely undisturbed. In 2014, the current Mushin Trust acquired the compound and undertook a five-year restoration using only craftsmen trained in traditional sukiya and shoin carpentry.

"We did not restore it to look old. We restored it to function as it always had — as a place where serious practice becomes possible."

— Takashi Morimoto, Founding Director

MUSHIN opened to its first residential group in Spring 2019. Each program since has been limited to twelve guests — not for exclusivity, but because the architecture of deep practice demands genuine intimacy.

What We Believe

The Three Pillars

Every program at MUSHIN is built on three interlocking convictions about what practice makes possible.

一 空 Emptiness Kū — The Open State

The full mind cannot receive. Before you can add anything — skill, understanding, transformation — you must first empty. This is not passive. Genuine emptiness requires tremendous courage and continuous attention.

二 型 Form Kata — The Container

We practice kata not to become mechanical, but to exhaust the mechanical. Repeated form eventually dissolves the commentary — the inner voice that narrates, judges, and avoids. What remains after the form is spontaneous.

三 間 Interval Ma — Negative Space

Japanese aesthetics prize the pause as much as the note. We schedule silence as deliberately as practice. The walk through the cedar grove, the twenty minutes before morning bell — these intervals are not empty. They are where integration happens.

Faculty

Masters in Residence

Sensei Kenji Nakamura Aikido · 7th Dan Aikikai · 44 Years Practice

Nakamura Sensei began his training under his father at age six in this same dojo. He spent eight years at the Aikikai Hombu in Tokyo before returning to teach at the temple. His technical lineage runs directly to Morihiro Saito's Iwama-ryu synthesis — a style emphasizing the deep relationship between empty-hand and weapons practice. In addition to Aikido, he holds a Shodo teacher's certificate and leads our morning calligraphy sessions on request.

Aikikai 7th Dan · Iaido 4th Dan · Shodo Kyoshi
Sensei Yuki Hayashi Iaido · Muso Shinden-ryu · 5th Dan ZNKR

Hayashi Sensei is the third generation of his family to practice iaido at this location — his grandfather taught here through the 1960s and 70s. He is the custodian of two late-Edo period blades used in demonstrating the school's classical forms. His curriculum is notable for stripping iaido back to its contemplative essence: the draw as a complete act, not merely a technique. He believes no one should hold a sword before they understand stillness.

ZNKR 5th Dan Iaido · AJKF Member · Tokyo National Museum Collaborator
Roshi Tomoko Abe Zazen Teacher · Rinzai Zen · Tenryū-ji Lineage

Roshi Abe ordained at Tenryū-ji at the age of 23, trained under three different Rinzai teachers across 20 years of intensive practice. She received Dharma transmission in 2012 and has been teaching dokusan — the intense one-on-one Zen interview — since 2015. Her instruction is without sentiment: she offers neither comfort nor encouragement, only presence and a mirror. Guests describe their sessions with her as the most demanding and clarifying experience of the program.

Rinzai Zen Dharma Heir · Author, "The Silence Before Bell" (2020)
