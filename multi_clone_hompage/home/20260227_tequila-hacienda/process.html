The Sacred Process

From Earth
to Glass

At Hacienda San Marcos, the path from agave plant to finished bottle involves no shortcuts, no diffusers, no artificial acceleration. Only the five steps that have defined tequila production in this hacienda for nearly a century.

01

Harvest — The Jimado

The agave harvest — called the jimado — begins before sunrise, when the highland air is cool and the light is golden. Our eleven jimadores move through the estate rows with the practiced efficiency of craftsmen who have spent their working lives in these fields. Using the coa de jima — a razor-sharp, disc-shaped blade on a long hardwood handle — each jimador strips the long pointed leaves (pencas) from the agave plant, revealing the dense, sugar-rich piña at the center.

A single mature piña weighs between 40 and 120 kilograms. The largest examples — the product of twelve or more years of growth — are treated with particular reverence. Only 100% mature plants are selected; any agave that does not meet our jimadores' standards is left to complete its cycle.

Duration: Seasonal harvest over 8–10 weeks | Tools: Coa de jima, hand forged | Agave age at harvest: 8–12 years

02

Roasting — The Hornos

Freshly harvested piñas are loaded into our traditional stone hornos — underground ovens sealed with large, flat volcanic stones. The piñas are slow-roasted using live steam for a minimum of 72 hours, a process that converts the complex carbohydrates (inulin) stored in the agave into fermentable sugars while simultaneously developing the complex roasted, caramel, and earthy flavors that define the AGAVERO profile.

The roasting method is critical. A diffuser — which uses hot water to extract the agave juice without cooking — is faster and cheaper, but it eliminates the Maillard reactions and caramelization processes that create depth of flavor. We have never used a diffuser and never will. Slow-roasting in stone hornos is non-negotiable.

Duration: 72 hours minimum | Method: Traditional stone horno, steam-roasted | Output: Golden roasted piña hearts ready for crushing

03

Crushing — The Tahona

After roasting, the golden piñas are split and fed beneath our tahona — a two-ton, wheel-shaped millstone carved from volcanic basalt. Pulled by a slow motor in a circular path, the tahona rolls continuously over the roasted agave, crushing the fibers and extracting the agave juice (mosto) without completely separating the solids from the liquid.

This is where the tahona method differs critically from roller mills. The basalt wheel crushes rather than shreds; it leaves the fiber (bagasse) intact and mixed with the juice. When this mixture ferments together, the fibers contribute additional complexity — a subtle, earthier dimension that is the unmistakable fingerprint of tahona tequila.

Duration: 4–6 hours per batch | Stone weight: 2 tons volcanic basalt | Origin: Quarried from Jalisco highlands, 1952

04

Fermentation — Open-Air Vats

The tahona mosto — agave juice mixed with crushed fiber — is transferred to our open-air cypress wood fermentation vats, where the real alchemy of tequila production occurs. We add nothing artificial to initiate fermentation. The wild yeasts native to our estate — present in the air, in the vats themselves, and on the agave fibers — begin the conversion of sugar to alcohol on their own schedule.

Our fermentation vats are the oldest working vessels in the Los Altos region. The cypress wood has been absorbing the microbial culture of this specific hacienda for decades; each vat carries a unique yeast community that contributes to the distinctive character of AGAVERO tequila. Commercial yeast strains produce predictable, consistent results. Ours produce something more interesting: the taste of this place.

Duration: 5–7 days | Vat material: Aged cypress wood | Yeast: Wild estate yeasts, no commercial strains | Result: Wash of 4–6% ABV

05

Distillation — Copper Pot Stills

The fermented wash undergoes double distillation in our original copper alembic pot stills — the same hand-hammered vessels that Don Aurelio purchased in 1928. The first distillation (destrozamiento) produces an ordinario of 20–30% ABV. The second distillation (rectificación) raises the spirit to 55–60% ABV and allows the maestro tequilero to make precise cuts — separating the heads and tails from the heart of the distillate.

Copper is not chosen for tradition alone. The metal actively reacts with sulfur compounds in the distillate, removing harsh, unpleasant notes and producing a cleaner, more refined spirit. Our copper stills are repaired and re-tinned annually — never replaced. The character they impart to the spirit after decades of use is considered an irreplaceable element of the AGAVERO profile.

Still type: Hand-hammered copper alembic | Distillation rounds: 2 | Final ABV from still: 55–60% | Age of primary stills: Est. 1928

The Still Room

Monuments to
the Craft

To step into the still room of Hacienda San Marcos is to step into 1928. The four original copper pot stills — their domed caps darkened with decades of use, their copper surfaces soft and burnished — stand like sentinels over the distillery floor. They have produced every liter of AGAVERO tequila that has ever been made.

These stills are not museum pieces. They run every working day of the production season, attended by distillery workers who have learned their rhythms over years of daily acquaintance. Each still has its own character — its own hot spots, its own cut timing — understood and accommodated by the team through accumulated experience rather than instrumentation.

The Barrel Hall

Where Time
Does Its Work

The barrel hall of Hacienda San Marcos holds 2,400 American white oak barrels at any given time — a three-acre stone building constructed in 1952 by Don Ernesto Agavero using volcanic stone quarried from the estate itself. The thick walls maintain a near-constant temperature and humidity throughout the year, creating ideal conditions for the slow alchemy of aging.

We use only 200-liter American white oak barrels, previously seasoned with American bourbon. The barrel's previous occupant imparts vanilla, caramel, and sweet wood notes that complement rather than overwhelm the agave spirit. Each barrel is inspected annually; those that no longer contribute positively to aging are retired.
