Methodology

Two Methods, One Standard

AURUM practises both water gilding and oil gilding. The choice between them is not aesthetic but technical — determined by the object's substrate, its environment, and the burnish level required. Water gilding produces the higher brilliance; oil gilding provides greater durability.

In all cases the preparation of the surface is the most demanding phase. Gold leaf is so thin — between 0.1 and 0.5 microns — that it follows every defect in the ground beneath it. The gilder's eye, therefore, is trained first for surface, not for gold.

"The gold reveals nothing except what you have prepared beneath it. A poor gesso will show through 23-karat leaf as surely as through a coat of paint — and it will show through for centuries."

— Édouard Merillon
I
Stage One

Gesso Preparation

All gilding begins with gesso — a liquid mixture of chalk (calcium carbonate) and warm rabbit-skin glue applied in multiple thin coats to the prepared wooden substrate. Each coat must be applied while the previous one is still slightly warm, to ensure adhesion, and allowed to harden fully before the next is added.

A traditionally prepared surface receives between eight and twelve coats of gesso, each abraded back when cured. The cumulative result is a chalky white surface of remarkable smoothness — smooth enough to reflect light before any gold is applied, which is the test that determines whether the gilder may proceed.

For restoration work, the existing gesso is examined under binocular microscopy before any decision about intervention. If the original gesso is structurally sound, we work over it. If it is failing, we consolidate it before deciding whether to apply new material.

Material
Chalk (calcium carbonate) — ground and sieved; rabbit-skin glue from European tanneries
Coats Applied
8–12 coats, each 0.2–0.4mm when wet, reducing to 0.05–0.1mm when cured and abraded
Curing Time
Minimum 3–5 days for full thickness; complex mouldings may require 10 days
Test for Completion
Surface reflects light evenly with no visible brushmarks when viewed at a raking angle under strong light
II
Stage Two

Bole Application

Bole is a coloured clay — traditionally Armenian bole, though French, Italian, and German sources are also used depending on the desired final appearance. It is mixed with dilute rabbit-skin glue and applied in two to four thin coats over the cured gesso, each burnished smooth with a soft cloth before the next is added.

The colour of the bole has a material effect on the final appearance of the gold. A red-brown bole (terra rossa) beneath burnished gold produces a warm, slightly orange glow; a yellow bole creates a lighter, more brilliant effect; a black bole gives depth and antiquity. The gilder chooses the bole colour not from convention alone but from careful observation of the object and the light in which it will be displayed.

Bole Colours
Terra rossa, yellow ochre, black, grey, and mixed tones — selected per commission
Coats Applied
2–4 coats, each burnished smooth when partially cured
Function
Creates the compressible foundation into which gold leaf is compressed during burnishing, producing high reflectivity
Effect on Finish
Red bole: warm gold with orange depth. Yellow bole: luminous, cool. Black bole: aged, depth, complexity
III
Stage Three

Sizing and Leaf Laying

For water gilding, the size is water itself — applied with a brush to the prepared bole surface until the clay is briefly softened and slightly adhesive. This window of adhesion lasts between five and twenty seconds depending on temperature and humidity. The gilder must lay the gold leaf within this window — too soon and the leaf sinks into a wet surface and loses brilliance; too late and it does not adhere at all.

Gold leaf — 0.1 to 0.5 microns in thickness, barely substantial enough to cast a shadow — is picked up from the gilding cushion with a gilder's tip (a flat brush edged with squirrel hair) and laid with a single gesture onto the sized surface. Breath control is not a metaphor: at this thickness, a normal exhale will carry the leaf across the room. The gilder works in controlled stillness.

For oil gilding, a mordant (slow-drying size mixed with oil) is applied and allowed to reach the correct level of tack — determined by touch — before the leaf is laid. The window here is measured in hours rather than seconds, which makes oil gilding more forgiving but its finish less brilliant.

Water Gilding Size
Cold water applied with a soft brush; adhesion window 5–20 seconds at 18°C and 55% RH
Oil Gilding Size
Japanners' gold size mixed to 4–8 hour open time; tack tested by knuckle touch
Leaf Thickness
23k leaf: 0.1–0.15 microns. 24k leaf: 0.12–0.18 microns. Double-weight leaf for restoration: 0.3–0.5 microns
Studio Conditions
Temperature: 17–19°C. Humidity: 52–58% RH. Air movement: nil — no windows open during leaf work
IV
Stage Four

Burnishing

Burnishing is the defining operation of water gilding — the act that distinguishes it from all other gilded finishes. An agate stone (traditionally a tooth of a carnelian or bloodstone, now usually a polished stone tip in a wooden handle) is drawn across the fully cured gold surface with firm, even pressure. The stone compresses the gold leaf into the clay bole beneath, creating a surface that reflects light as a near-perfect mirror.

The distinction between burnished and unburnished areas of a gilded surface — a technique known as "matteing," used to create contrast on carved frames — is one of the most sophisticated effects in the gilder's vocabulary. The matt areas are left lightly sealed but not burnished; the burnished areas catch and throw light with an intensity that flat paint cannot approach.

Oil-gilded surfaces cannot be burnished to the same degree — the oil mordant does not create the same compressible clay cushion. Their finish is satin to semi-gloss rather than mirror. This is often appropriate for furniture intended for daily use, where a perfect burnish would show handling marks.

Tool
Agate burnisher — polished semi-precious stone in turned boxwood handle; several profiles for different surface geometries
Timing
Water gilding: burnished 4–24 hours after laying depending on season. Too soon: leaf tears. Too late: no adhesion to bole, no mirror finish
Pressure
Applied by feel — pressure varies across curved surfaces, increases as burnish develops, adjusted by ear (the sound of the stone on gold changes as mirror finish emerges)
Finish Options
Full burnish (mirror), partial burnish with matt, antiqued (burnished then distressed), or left fully matt (unusual, for specific period effects)
Methods Compared

Water Gilding vs. Oil Gilding

The choice of method is determined by the object and its use. Both methods use genuine gold leaf — the difference is in the size, the adhesion mechanism, and therefore the final surface quality.

Water Gilding
Water Gilding
Traditional — Museum Standard
  • —Produces mirror-bright burnished finish unavailable by any other method
  • —Traditional method: unchanged since the fifteenth century
  • —Requires bole ground — surface must be prepared to high standard
  • —Sensitive to humidity during laying — requires controlled studio conditions
  • —Best for: frames, sacred objects, museum-grade furniture, showcases
  • —Not suitable for outdoor use or surfaces subject to handling
Oil Gilding
Oil Gilding
Durable — Versatile
  • —Satin to semi-gloss finish; cannot be burnished to mirror brilliance
  • —More durable: suitable for furniture that will be handled and used
  • —Can be applied to a wider range of substrates including metal and plaster
  • —Larger working window than water gilding — more forgiving to apply
  • —Best for: furniture in daily use, exterior architectural elements, large-scale interiors
  • —Can be sealed for exterior durability with appropriate protective coatings
Traditional Materials

What We Use and Why

Modern synthetic alternatives exist for almost every traditional gilding material. We do not use them. Traditional materials perform better over time, age more gracefully, and are reversible — the standard of museum conservation practice.

Rabbit-Skin Glue
Traditional European — bovine hide alternative available

The binding agent for both gesso and bole. Protein-based, reversible with warm water. Used unchanged since antiquity. We source from a single French supplier.

Natural Chalk
Champagne, France — finest particle size

Calcium carbonate mined from the chalk strata of the Champagne region and ground to micron consistency. The basis of all our gesso work.

Armenian Bole
Sourced from Turkey, Germany, and France

Coloured clay used as the final layer before gold. Each colour has a distinct effect on the final gold tone. We stock eight bole colours and mix to commission.

23k Double Gold Leaf
Blattgold Eytzinger, Germany; Giusto Manetti, Italy

The finest gold leaf available, produced by beating pure gold alloy to a thickness of 0.12–0.15 microns. Sourced from two European suppliers with unbroken production lineage.

Agate Burnishers
Custom-made from semi-precious stone blanks

Polished agate tips in turned boxwood handles, made to the gilder's specification. Each stone is dressed and polished in-house. A gilder's burnisher is a personal tool, never shared.

Renaissance Wax
Picreator Enterprises, London

A microcrystalline protective wax applied as the final seal on completed work. Invisible, reversible, and the international museum standard for protective coatings on gilded surfaces.
